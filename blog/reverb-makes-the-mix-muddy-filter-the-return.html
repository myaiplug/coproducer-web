Reverb making the mix muddy? Filter the return, not the vocal

NoDAW Labs · Lacquer Vol. 05

When reverb makes a mix muddy, the fix is usually on the return, not the vocal: a dark hall puts most of its energy below 1 kHz and keeps ringing there in the gaps between notes. High-pass the return around 250–400 Hz, give it enough pre-delay that the dry attack lands first, and time the decay to the song so the tail clears before the next line.

Here’s how it usually goes. The vocal sounds great soloed with its reverb. Then the beat comes in and the whole middle of the mix turns to fog. So you start cutting the vocal at 300 Hz, then the keys, then you try a different reverb, and somehow the vocal ends up thin and the mix is still cloudy.

The vocal was never the problem. Mute the reverb return and the fog goes with it. What you’re hearing is the reverb’s low-mids, smeared across time and parked in exactly the spaces the dry parts used to leave open. So fix it where it lives: on the return.

Why the mud lives in the return

A reverb return is a copy of everything you send it, stretched over a second or three. Two things make that copy heavier in the low-mids than the source.

  • Your sends are bottom-heavy. A sung vocal carries a lot of its energy in the fundamentals and first few harmonics, roughly 150–500 Hz for most voices. Keys, guitars and snare body live there too. Send three things to one hall and you get all three, blended.
  • Lows ring longer. Most halls and plates (real or simulated) decay more slowly at the bottom. Plugins often expose this as a bass multiplier or low decay control. So the low-mid part of the tail is the part still hanging around when the next word starts.

Average it over a whole chorus and the extra low-mid looks tiny, which is why neither a meter nor an EQ on the mix bus flags it. The damage happens in the gaps: between syllables, between chord hits, in the half-beat before a downbeat. Those little dips are what make a mix sound open. Reverb fills them in.

We measured it on a synthetic ballad

To put numbers on it we built a test session at 48 kHz and 75 BPM: a vocal-like line (harmonics, formants, vibrato and short consonant bursts), piano-ish keys on every beat and a bass. The vocal and the keys (6 dB lower) both feed one synthetic stereo hall with a 2.8 s low decay, 2.0 s mids and 1.0 s highs. The return is set once, unfiltered, 8 dB under the dry vocal, and stays at that gain for every row unless it says level-matched. Synthetic test signals, so trust the shape more than the decimals.

On its own, that unfiltered return put 86% of its energy below 1 kHz and 13% in the 1–6 kHz range where you actually hear space and distance.

Gap fill is how much the return raises the mix’s 150–500 Hz energy in the short gaps between sung notes. Low-mid contrast is how much that band still rises and falls from moment to moment (the spread between loud and quiet 25 ms frames); lower means more filled in. Return 1–6 kHz and Return level are relative to the unfiltered return. Low-mid tail is the return’s 150–500 Hz energy half a beat to a beat and a half after the last note, relative to the dry vocal’s. Filters are 12 dB per octave unless marked.

Three things to take from that table into a session:

  • The mud is in the gaps, not the average. Across the whole phrase, the unfiltered return lifted the mix’s 150–500 Hz band by only 0.6 dB. In the gaps between notes it lifted it by 4.3 dB, and the band lost about 2 dB of its movement. That’s the cloudiness, and it’s why EQ on the mix bus never quite fixes it.
  • Filtering costs you almost none of the space. A 350 Hz high-pass halved the gap fill, dropped the leftover low-mid tail by 9 dB and left the 1–6 kHz part of the reverb exactly where it was. The return as a whole got 1.7 dB quieter, nearly all of it mud. Push the fader back up by that much and the gaps are still cleaner than before (+2.9 dB against +4.3), with the audible space 1.6 dB louder.
  • The default low cut is aimed at rumble, not mud. A 150 Hz cut barely moved anything (+4.1 dB). Below about 250 Hz you’re cleaning sub rumble, which matters too, just not for this.

The bottom two rows are the other lever. Halving the low decay alone took the leftover tail from −31.5 to −49.8 dB, and with the filter it fell to −56.9: 25 dB less low-mid hanging over the next line, while the 1–6 kHz part moved by 0.1 dB. If your reverb has a bass multiplier or a low-frequency damping control, it is a mud control.

Pre-delay keeps the front of the word dry

Pre-delay doesn’t clean the low-mids (in this test the mix’s 150–500 Hz rise stayed between 0.0 and 0.3 dB whatever the pre-delay). What it buys is articulation. With the reverb arriving a beat-fraction late, the consonant and the attack of each note reach your ear dry, and the wash follows.

Once the pre-delay is longer than the attack you care about, the front of the note is effectively dry. Past that point more pre-delay stops helping the consonant and starts sounding like a slap. Somewhere between 20 and 60 ms is where most sung vocals land. Syncing it to a small note value keeps it from feeling rhythmically off.

Duck the return off the dry vocal

Put a compressor (or a ducker) on the reverb return and key its sidechain from the dry vocal. The reverb sinks while the singer sings and blooms in the spaces. We used a gentle setting: 6 dB at most, 5 ms attack, 250 ms release, on the filtered return with 25 ms of pre-delay.

  • Gain reduction averaged 5.4 dB while singing and 1.8 dB in the gaps and tail.
  • While the vocal was sounding, the return went from 10.4 dB under the dry vocal to 15.9 dB under it. That’s the clarity.
  • The tail in the gaps came out 3.6 dB lower than without ducking, because a 250 ms release is still recovering when the line ends. Shorten the release if you want the bloom back sooner.

Ducking doesn’t change the low-mid average much (+0.3 to +0.1 dB in this test). It’s a front-to-back tool: the vocal stays in front, the reverb still reads as big, it just stops talking over the singer.

Time the decay to the song

A tail that’s still ringing when the next phrase starts is mud you added on purpose. A quick way to get a starting point is to let the tempo pick the numbers:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""reverb_times.py: tempo-synced pre-delay and decay starting points.
    python3 reverb_times.py 72            # one tempo
    python3 reverb_times.py 72 140 120 85 # several
Decay is the note length minus the pre-delay, so the tail is done by the time
that note value has passed. These are starting points: set them, then listen."""
import sys

def note_ms(bpm, frac):                  # frac of a whole note, e.g. 1/16
    return 60000 / bpm * 4 * frac

for bpm in map(float, sys.argv[1:] or ["72"]):
    print(f"{bpm:g} BPM  (quarter = {note_ms(bpm, 1/4):.0f} ms)")
    pre = {n: note_ms(bpm, f) for n, f in (("1/128", 1/128), ("1/64", 1/64), ("1/32", 1/32))}
    print("  pre-delay  " + "   ".join(f"{n} = {v:.0f} ms" for n, v in pre.items()))
    for name, frac in (("1/4 note", 1/4), ("1/2 note", 1/2), ("1 bar", 1), ("2 bars", 2)):
        total = note_ms(bpm, frac)
        print(f"  decay to clear in {name:8s} {total/1000:5.2f} s total -> "
              f"{(total - pre['1/64'])/1000:4.2f} s decay with 1/64 pre-delay")

The decay knob on most reverbs is a 60 dB decay time (RT60). In a busy mix the last 30 dB or so of that tail is masked anyway, so a decay set to one bar sounds like it clears well before the bar ends. Set it from the table, then adjust by ear with the whole mix playing, not soloed.

Check what your return is actually doing

Solo and bounce the dry vocal and the reverb return (100% wet, same start). This shows where the return puts its energy next to the dry part:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""wet_dry_bands.py: where does your reverb return put its energy, next to the dry vocal?
Solo and bounce the dry vocal and the reverb return (return only, 100% wet), same start, then:
    python3 wet_dry_bands.py vocal_dry.wav reverb_return.wav"""
import sys, subprocess, numpy as np

def load(path):  # any format ffmpeg reads -> stereo float at 48 kHz (mono files are copied to both sides)
    raw = subprocess.run(["ffmpeg", "-v", "error", "-i", path, "-ac", "2", "-ar", "48000", "-f", "f32le", "-"],
                         capture_output=True, check=True).stdout
    return np.frombuffer(raw, np.float32).reshape(-1, 2).astype(np.float64)

def power(x, n):  # power spectrum summed over both channels (a stereo return is not folded to mono)
    return (np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x[:n], axis=0)) ** 2).sum(axis=1)

dry, wet = load(sys.argv[1]), load(sys.argv[2])
n = min(len(dry), len(wet)); f = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, 1 / 48000)
D, W = power(dry, n), power(wet, n)
bands = [("sub 20-150", 20, 150), ("low-mid 150-500", 150, 500), ("mid 500-1k", 500, 1000),
         ("presence 1-6k", 1000, 6000), ("air 6-16k", 6000, 16000)]
print(f"{'band':16s} {'return vs dry':>13s} {'share of return':>16s}")
for name, lo, hi in bands:
    m = (f >= lo) & (f < hi)
    ratio = f"{10*np.log10(W[m].sum() / D[m].sum()):+11.1f} dB" if D[m].sum() > 1e-4 * D.sum() else "   (dry ~none)"
    print(f"{name:16s} {ratio} {100 * W[m].sum() / W.sum():14.0f} %")
print(f"{'broadband':16s} {10*np.log10(W.sum() / D.sum()):+11.1f} dB")

The low-mid share of the return fell from 43% to 24%, and the presence band (−8.8 dB against the dry vocal) didn’t change at all. If your own return shows 150–500 Hz as its biggest band, that’s where to start.

No session, just a bounce? Splitting the vocal out with StemSplit and listening to it on its own gives you a decent feel for how much wash got printed. Separators don’t always agree on where a tail belongs, so use it as a listening aid, not a measurement.

Fold it to mono once

A plain stereo reverb is mostly decorrelated left to right (our return measured −0.09). Fold the mix to mono and it gets quieter relative to the centred vocal: 3.4 dB quieter here. So mono doesn’t make a normal reverb muddier. It makes it drier, which is worth knowing if most people will hear the song on a phone. If your return gets louder or hollower in mono, something on it, a widener or a polarity trick, is fighting itself. Wide mixes fail in mono covers that check.

Genre notes

  • R&B ballad vocal (around 68 BPM). Big halls and plates are part of the sound, so keep the length and take the mud out: high-pass at 300–400 Hz, pre-delay near 1/64 (55 ms), decay around a bar (3.5 s) and a few dB of ducking. Stacks and harmonies can share the lead’s return; don’t give every layer its own hall.
  • Trap vocal (140 BPM, half-time feel). Dense 808s leave no room for a long tail. Short plate or room, decay around a half note (0.8 s), high-pass at 400 Hz or higher and duck it hard. Let delay throws on the last word carry the space.
  • Rock snare (around 120 BPM). A plate or room on the snare is where a lot of the body comes from, so be gentler: high-pass around 200–250 Hz and time the decay to land by the next kick, about a quarter note (0.47 s). A gate on the return instead of a long natural decay is a classic move for a reason.
  • Lo-fi (around 85 BPM). Dark reverb is the aesthetic, so low-pass lower (4–5 kHz) but still high-pass it. The warmth should come from the dry keys, not from a 300 Hz tail sitting on them. A decay of about a half note (1.4 s) keeps the loop breathing.
Filter the return, pre-delay it, duck it, time it, in that order, and the reverb goes back to sounding like space instead of fog. If the vocal itself still feels buried, read how loud the vocal should sit. If the reverb is spreading sibilance around the room, de-ess before the send. Got a bounce but no session? Pull the vocal with StemSplit and listen.
Why does reverb make my mix muddy?
Because a reverb return is mostly low-mid energy that keeps ringing after the dry note stops. On our synthetic ballad, an unfiltered hall raised the mix’s 150–500 Hz band by only 0.6 dB on average but by 4.3 dB in the gaps between notes, which is where clarity lives.
What frequency should I high-pass a vocal reverb at?
Start around 250–400 Hz on the return, not the vocal. In our test a 350 Hz high-pass halved the low-mid fill in the gaps and cut the leftover tail by 9 dB, while the 1–6 kHz part of the reverb didn’t change. A 150 Hz cut barely helped.
How much pre-delay should vocal reverb have?
Enough that the start of each word lands dry, typically 20–60 ms. In our test 25 ms raised the dry-over-wet ratio in the first 30 ms of each note from 14.8 to 18.3 dB, and 50 ms made it effectively dry. Sync it to a 1/128 or 1/64 note to keep it in time.
Does reverb get muddier in mono?
A plain stereo reverb gets quieter in mono, not muddier. Our return was almost fully decorrelated, and folding to mono dropped it 3.4 dB relative to the centred vocal. If your return gets louder or hollower in mono, something on it is out of polarity between the two sides.
