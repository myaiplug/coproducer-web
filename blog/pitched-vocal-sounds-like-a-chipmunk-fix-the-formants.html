Pitched-up vocal sounds like a chipmunk? It’s the formants, not the pitch

NoDAW Labs · Lacquer Vol. 06

A pitched-up vocal sounds like a chipmunk because the formants, the resonances of the singer’s throat and mouth that make a voice sound like that person, move up along with the pitch. In our test a 4-semitone shift moved a vowel’s three formants up about 24 to 27% with both varispeed and a plain pitch shifter, and down about 16 to 24% going 4 semitones down. A formant-kept version of the same notes moved them 3% or less. Use a shifter with formant preservation, or shift the formants back on purpose.

You flip a vocal sample up a few semitones to fit your beat and suddenly it’s a cartoon. Or you slow a hook down for that screwed feel and the singer turns into a giant. The notes are right. The person sounds wrong.

That’s because two things carry a voice: the note it’s singing and the shape of the throat and mouth singing it. Shift a sample the simple way and you move both.

What formants are

When someone sings, their vocal cords make a buzz full of harmonics. Their throat, mouth and nose then boost a few frequency ranges and cut others. Those boosted ranges are the formants. They stay roughly put for a given vowel and a given person, whatever note is sung, and they’re a big part of how you recognize a voice and tell an “ah” from an “ee.”

Varispeed, the tape or turntable kind of pitch change, scales every frequency by the same ratio, so formants go wherever the pitch goes. Plenty of pitch shifters do the same thing to keep the length the same. A smaller voice has higher formants, so pitched-up formants read as a smaller person: chipmunk. Pitched-down ones read as a bigger person: monster.

We measured it in formant_check.py. It builds an “ah” vowel with formants at 730, 1090 and 2440 Hz, shifts it 4 semitones each way with resampling and with librosa’s plain pitch shift, and makes a formant-kept version by singing the new note through the same throat, which is the result a formant-preserving shifter aims for. Then it reads the formants back with LPC, the standard speech-analysis method. Our reader comes in a few percent off on the original (766 Hz for the 730 Hz formant), so compare each shift against that original reading.

# Where a vowel's formants land after pitch shifting: varispeed, a plain pitch shifter, and a formant-kept version.
# Synthetic "ah" vowel (glottal-style pulse train through three formant resonators), fixed seed.
import warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
import numpy as np, scipy.signal as sg, librosa
fs = 16000; rng = np.random.default_rng(17)
FORMANTS = [(730, 90), (1090, 110), (2440, 170)]           # textbook adult "ah": centre Hz, bandwidth Hz

def vowel(f0, dur=1.5):
    t = np.arange(int(fs * dur)) / fs
    ph = 2 * np.pi * np.cumsum(f0 * (1 + 0.005 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 5 * t))) / fs
    src = sum(np.sin(k * ph) / k ** 1.2 for k in range(1, int(7000 / f0)))   # bright, falling source
    src += 0.003 * rng.standard_normal(t.size)
    y = src
    for fc, bw in FORMANTS:                                   # cascade of two-pole resonators
        r = np.exp(-np.pi * bw / fs); th = 2 * np.pi * fc / fs
        y = sg.lfilter([1 - r], [1, -2 * r * np.cos(th), r * r], y)
    return y / np.max(np.abs(y)) * 0.5

def formants(y):
    """First three formants from LPC roots on the middle of the clip."""
    seg = y[len(y) // 4: 3 * len(y) // 4] * np.hamming(len(y) // 2)
    seg = sg.lfilter([1, -0.63], [1], seg)                    # pre-emphasis, as speech tools do
    a = librosa.lpc(seg, order=14)
    roots = [r for r in np.roots(a) if np.imag(r) > 0]
    cand = sorted((np.angle(r) * fs / (2 * np.pi), -fs / np.pi * np.log(abs(r))) for r in roots)
    return [f for f, bw in cand if f > 250 and bw < 500][:3]

def varispeed(y, semis):                                      # tape/turntable style: pitch and speed together
    return sg.resample(y, int(round(len(y) / 2 ** (semis / 12))))

def show(name, y, ref=None):
    f = formants(y)
    line = f"   {name:40s} F1 {f[0]:6.0f} Hz  F2 {f[1]:6.0f} Hz  F3 {f[2]:6.0f} Hz"
    if ref: line += "  | moved " + " ".join(f"{100 * (a / b - 1):+5.1f}%" for a, b in zip(f, ref))
    print(line); return f

f0 = 130.0
print(f"Vowel 'ah' sung at {f0:.0f} Hz; formants built in at " + ", ".join(f"{fc} Hz" for fc, _ in FORMANTS))
ref = show("original", vowel(f0))
for semis in (+4, -4):
    print(f"\n{semis:+d} semitones (pitch ratio {2 ** (semis / 12):.3f})")
    show("varispeed / resample", varispeed(vowel(f0), semis), ref)
    show("plain pitch shift (librosa, no formants)", librosa.effects.pitch_shift(vowel(f0), sr=fs, n_steps=semis), ref)
    show("formants kept (same throat, new note)", vowel(f0 * 2 ** (semis / 12)), ref)

print("\nHow far formants move per shift with varispeed or a plain shifter:")
for semis in (1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 12, -3, -5, -7):
    print(f"   {semis:+3d} st -> formants {100 * (2 ** (semis / 12) - 1):+6.1f}%")

Reading the numbers

Up 4 semitones, the whole voice moved up a quarter. The pitch ratio for 4 semitones is 1.260, and that’s what happened to the formants: up 23.7 to 25.5% with varispeed and 23.7 to 27.0% with the plain pitch shifter. The plain shifter kept the length but did nothing for the voice. The formant-kept version moved its formants 0.0 to 2.0%, inside our reader’s error.

Down 4 semitones, everything dropped about a fifth. Varispeed moved the formants down 19.5 to 20.2% and the plain shifter 16.1 to 23.5%. The formant-kept version stayed within 2.6%. That drop is the big, dark, slowed-down character of a screwed vocal, and that’s why it can sound great on purpose and wrong by accident.

Small shifts are already noticeable. With varispeed or a plain shifter, 1 semitone moves formants 5.9% and 3 semitones moves them 18.9%. At +5 it’s 33.5%, and a full octave doubles them. Down, −3 is −15.9% and −7 is −33.3%. Past two or three semitones most people start hearing a different singer.

What to do in your session

  1. If the vocal needs to sound like the same person in a new key, use a pitch shifter with formant preservation turned on. Most DAW stock shifters and dedicated vocal tuners have the switch; it’s often labelled formant, preserve formants or similar.
  2. For a big shift, split the difference. Many shifters let you move formants separately. Shift pitch by the full amount and formants by a smaller amount, so the voice changes a little but doesn’t turn into a cartoon.
  3. If you want the chipmunk or the monster, own it. Varispeed is the classic sound for soul chops sped up and screwed hooks slowed down. Pick it on purpose rather than living with it.
  4. Shift the vocal on its own when you can. Formant tools work best on one clean voice; on a full mix they also bend drums and instruments. Pull the vocal out of the sample first, shift it, then put it back over the original or re-pitched beat.
  5. Check the key after. A 4-semitone shift is a ratio of 1.260 either way; the formant setting changes the color, not the note.

If you’re slowing a whole song down for a screwed version, that deep voice is the point, and that’s the sound ScrewAI is built around. When it’s just a vocal chop that needs to fit a new key and still sound like the singer, separate it first and use a formant-aware shifter on the vocal alone.

Formant shifting with the pitch left alone is its own trick. Nudging formants down a few percent can make a thin vocal sound fuller, and nudging them up can make a double sound like a different singer.
Why does my pitched-up vocal sound like a chipmunk?
The formants moved up with the pitch. In our test 4 semitones up moved a vowel’s formants up about 24 to 27% with both varispeed and a plain pitch shifter.
How do I pitch shift a vocal without the chipmunk sound?
Use a pitch shifter with formant preservation on. Our formant-kept version of the same 4-semitone shift moved the formants 3% or less.
Why do slowed-down vocals sound so deep?
Slowing a recording lowers formants as well as pitch. Four semitones down moved our vowel’s formants down about 20% with varispeed.
How many semitones can you shift a vocal before it sounds unnatural?
It depends on the voice, but with plain shifting formants move 5.9% per semitone up and 18.9% at 3 semitones, and past two or three semitones most people hear a different singer.
