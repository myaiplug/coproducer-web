How to make a slowed version of your song: slow the bounce, or go back to the session?

NoDAW Labs · Lacquer

Make a slowed version from the finished bounce when the mix already works and you want everything to drop together. Go back to the session when slowing breaks something only the session can fix, like an 808 that sinks below what speakers can play or a vocal that has to stay in key. Either way, measure the slowed file again before it ships.

Slowed versions aren’t a niche thing anymore. The sound grew out of Houston’s chopped and screwed tapes, and the slowed + reverb edits that followed made it a normal part of a release for a lot of artists. So the question usually isn’t whether to make one. It’s where in the process to make it, and how to keep it from quietly falling apart on a phone.

Should you slow the finished bounce or the session?

Most of the time, the bounce. If you like the mix, slowing the stereo file keeps every decision you already made: the balance, the bus glue, the way the vocal sits. That’s also what gives the slowed sound its character. Everything moves down together, the way a tape or turntable does when it runs slow.

The session wins when slowing exposes a problem you can’t fix in a stereo file. A quick way to decide:

  • Slow the bounce when the mix is finished, you want the classic slower-and-lower sound, and the low end still reads on small speakers after slowing.
  • Go back to the session when the 808 or bass disappears, when the vocal needs to stay in its original key, when the hats turn into mush, or when the mix itself still has masking or phase problems.

That last point matters more than it sounds. Slowing doesn’t fix a mix. A muddy low-mid or a vocal sitting in the wrong spot is still there at 0.8x, just longer. If you’re still hunting a frequency, stay in the session and finish the mix first.

What does slowing actually do to a mix you trust?

It depends on which kind of slowing you use, and that choice is the whole game:

  • Tape-style slowing (varispeed) plays the same samples back slower. Tempo and pitch drop together by the same ratio. At 0.8x the song drops 3.86 semitones; at 0.5x it drops exactly an octave. This is the classic slowed and screwed sound.
  • Time-stretching slows the tempo and keeps the pitch where it was. It’s a different algorithm, it can smear transients, and it sounds less like a record and more like a stretched file. Time stretching and pitch scaling covers the methods if you want the deep version.

We measured the difference between those modes in detail in resampling vs pitch shift vs tempo change. For a slowed version, what you’ll hear on a tape-style pass is this: kicks land later and rounder, hats get darker and lose their snap, reverb tails stretch out, and a vocal that sat proud can start to feel late and heavy. That’s usually the point. It’s also why you do it on a bounce you already trust, so the only thing that changed is the speed.

Why does the 808 disappear in the slowed version?

Because every frequency in the song is divided by the same ratio. Here’s the math on a 45 Hz 808, which is a pretty normal fundamental:

  • at 0.9x it becomes 40.5 Hz,
  • at 0.8x it becomes 36 Hz,
  • at 0.5x (true halftime) it becomes 22.5 Hz.

At 22.5 Hz you’re near the bottom edge of human hearing and well below what most earbuds and phone speakers reproduce with any real level. The energy is still in the file, and it still uses up headroom, but listeners hear the 808 vanish and the kick turn into a soft thud. If your slowed version sounds thin on a phone even though the meter looks full, this is usually why.

That’s the clearest sign to go back to the session. There you can pitch the 808 up before the bounce, lean on its harmonics with a little saturation so it reads on small speakers, or simply slow the song less. If you keep the stereo bounce, a gentle high-pass well below the new fundamental takes out the sub rumble nobody can hear and gives the headroom back.

How much should you slow it?

There’s no correct number, and anyone who gives you one is guessing about your song. A sensible way to find it:

  1. Start modest, around 0.85x, and listen to the hook first.
  2. Check it on earbuds and a phone speaker, not only on monitors. That’s where the low end gives up first.
  3. Go slower only while the vocal still feels like a person and the drums still feel like a groove.
  4. Write down the exact ratio you used, so you can repeat it for the rest of a project.

True halftime (0.5x) is a much bigger move than it sounds. A full octave down turns most vocals into something closer to a sound effect. That can be great for an interlude or a screwed tape feel, but it’s rarely what people mean by a slowed version of a single.

What changes on the meter after you slow it?

More than you’d guess, and not always in the direction you’d guess. Loudness meters following ITU-R BS.1770 weight frequencies: deep lows count for less than mids. When slowing pushes energy down into the lows, the song can read quieter even though nothing got turned down. True peak can move too, because the waveform between the samples changes shape. So we measured it. The script below takes a finished bounce, makes three slowed versions with FFmpeg’s filters (asetrate and aresample for tape-style slowing, atempo for time-stretching), and prints duration, integrated loudness, true peak, and how much of the energy sits below 30 Hz. You need FFmpeg plus Python with numpy and soundfile.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""slow_check.py: make slowed versions of a finished bounce and re-measure them.
Usage: python3 slow_check.py bounce.wav
Needs FFmpeg on PATH. Writes three files next to the input:
  *_vari080.wav  varispeed 0.8x (tape-style: slower AND lower; asetrate + aresample)
  *_vari050.wav  varispeed 0.5x (halftime by resampling: twice as long, one octave down)
  *_tempo080.wav time-stretch 0.8x (slower, same pitch; FFmpeg atempo)
Then prints duration, integrated loudness (LUFS) and true peak (dBTP) for each,
using FFmpeg's ebur128 filter (ITU-R BS.1770), plus the share of energy below 30 Hz
(where a lot of small speakers and earbuds give up). Needs numpy and soundfile too."""
import sys, re, math, subprocess
import numpy as np, soundfile as sf
from pathlib import Path

def run(args): return subprocess.run(args, capture_output=True, text=True).stderr

def measure(p):
    err = run(["ffmpeg", "-hide_banner", "-nostats", "-i", str(p), "-af", "ebur128=peak=true", "-f", "null", "-"])
    I = float(re.findall(r"I:\s+(-?[\d.]+) LUFS", err)[-1])
    tp = float(re.findall(r"Peak:\s+(-?[\d.]+) dBFS", err)[-1])
    d = re.search(r"Duration: (\d+):(\d+):([\d.]+)", err).groups()
    return int(d[0]) * 3600 + int(d[1]) * 60 + float(d[2]), I, tp

def sub30(p):
    x, fs = sf.read(str(p), always_2d=True); m = x.mean(1)
    P = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(m)) ** 2; f = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(m), 1 / fs)
    return 100 * P[f < 30].sum() / P.sum()

src = Path(sys.argv[1]); sr = 48000
jobs = [("original", None, 1.0, 0.0),
        ("vari080", f"asetrate={int(sr*0.8)},aresample={sr}", 0.8, 12 * math.log2(0.8)),
        ("vari050", f"asetrate={int(sr*0.5)},aresample={sr}", 0.5, 12 * math.log2(0.5)),
        ("tempo080", "atempo=0.8", 0.8, 0.0)]
print(f"{'version':<10}{'speed':>7}{'pitch':>9}{'length':>9}{'LUFS':>8}{'dBTP':>8}{'<30Hz':>8}")
base = None
for name, af, speed, semis in jobs:
    out = src if af is None else src.with_name(f"{src.stem}_{name}.wav")
    if af: run(["ffmpeg", "-v", "error", "-y", "-i", str(src), "-af", af, "-c:a", "pcm_f32le", str(out)])
    dur, I, tp = measure(out)
    print(f"{name:<10}{speed:>6.2f}x{semis:>+8.2f}st{dur:>8.1f}s{I:>8.1f}{tp:>+8.1f}{sub30(out):>7.1f}%" + ("  <- over -1 dBTP" if tp > -1.0 else ""))

Here’s what it printed on a synthetic test beat, not a real song: kick, snare, hats, a pad and a sine bass with roots between 41 and 55 Hz, capped at about −1.2 dBTP. The material is generated by make_material.py in the demo folder, so you can reproduce it exactly.

$ python3 slow_check.py bounce.wav
version     speed    pitch   length    LUFS    dBTP   <30Hz
original    1.00x   +0.00st    20.5s   -10.8    -1.2    0.2%
vari080     0.80x   -3.86st    25.6s   -11.9    -1.2    0.4%
vari050     0.50x  -12.00st    41.0s   -14.7    -1.2   79.3%
tempo080    0.80x   +0.00st    25.6s   -11.0    -1.3    1.2%

Three things stand out:

  • The slowed versions read quieter. 0.8x tape-style came in 1.1 LU under the original, and halftime came in 3.9 LU under. The time-stretched version barely moved (0.2 LU), because its spectrum stayed where it was.
  • Halftime moved most of the energy below 30 Hz. On this beat, 79% of the energy in the halftime file sits under 30 Hz, up from 0.2%. That’s the disappearing 808, measured. It still costs headroom; it just doesn’t sound like anything on a phone.
  • True peak held on this material. It stayed around −1.2 dBTP in every version. That’s this beat, not a rule. Real songs with bright transients, reverb added afterwards, or a loudness push to match the original can and do go over, so measure every slowed file.

The quieter reading also tempts people into a mistake: pushing the slowed version back up to match the original’s number. Since streaming services normalize playback anyway (the essay on why streaming turns loud masters down explains how), chasing the same LUFS mostly spends headroom. Match by ear, then check that the peak is legal.

A slowed-version checklist

  1. Finish the mix first. Slowing won’t fix masking, phase or a vocal in the wrong chair.
  2. Pick the mode on purpose: tape-style for the classic sound, time-stretch only when the key has to stay put.
  3. Do the 808 math for your ratio. If the fundamental lands under about 30 Hz, consider going back to the session.
  4. Bounce at full quality and keep the original bounce next to the slowed export for A/B.
  5. Re-measure loudness and true peak on the slowed file, and cap it again if it’s over.
  6. Name it clearly (slowed, 0.85x, date), so the right file reaches the distributor.

Should I slow down the master or the session?
Slow the finished bounce when the mix already works and you want the classic slowed sound, where everything drops in pitch together. Go back to the session when slowing breaks something you can only fix there: an 808 that sinks below what speakers can play, a vocal that needs to stay in its key, or a balance that only worked at the original tempo.
How much should I slow a song for a slowed version?
There’s no correct number. Start modest, somewhere around 0.85x, and listen on earbuds and a phone speaker before you go further. At 0.8x tape-style slowing the whole song drops almost four semitones; at 0.5x (true halftime) it drops a full octave and the low end can fall out of range.
Why does my 808 disappear in the slowed version?
Because tape-style slowing lowers every frequency by the same ratio. A 45 Hz 808 becomes 36 Hz at 0.8x and 22.5 Hz at 0.5x, which a lot of earbuds and phone speakers barely reproduce. Either slow it less, or rebuild the low end in the session so it sits higher after slowing.
Do I need to re-master a slowed version?
Treat it as its own file. Measure loudness and true peak again before you upload, because both can move. In our synthetic example the slowed versions read 1 to 4 LU quieter, while true peak held; your song can go either way, so measure rather than assume.
Is making a slowed version a mastering step?
No. It’s a creative print of a mix you already trust. Make the version you want to hear, then master or at least check the file you’ll actually distribute.
