What key is this sample in? Treat the key detector as a shortlist

NoDAW Labs · Lacquer Vol. 06

To find the key of a sample, use a key detector for a shortlist, then decide by ear which note the loop keeps coming home to, and check whether it’s tuned between keys. In our test a detector called an Am–F–C–G loop C major and ranked A minor seventh for an Am–Dm–Em–Am loop. A loop slowed to 90% came out 18 cents sharp of the A# major the detector named, so an 808 tuned straight to A# would sit slightly flat under it.

You chop a loop, it sounds great, and now it needs an 808 and maybe some keys. So you drop it into a key detector, it says C major, you program a bass line in C, and something feels off. Not wrong exactly. Just not home.

Key detectors are useful. They’re also guessing, and they guess wrong in a few predictable ways. Once you know those ways, the detector becomes a fast shortlist instead of a final answer.

How a key detector decides

Most detectors do some version of the same thing. They add up how much energy lands on each of the twelve note names across the sample, compare that pattern with a template for every major and minor key, and pick the best match. We built a small one in key_check.py using the classic Krumhansl–Kessler key profiles, and fed it loops made of plain harmonic tones so we know exactly what’s in them. It also measures how many cents the notes sit off the standard A440 grid.

# Find the key of a sample: pitch-class energy + Krumhansl-Kessler key profiles. Synthetic loop, fixed seed.
import numpy as np
fs = 44100; NAMES = "C C# D D# E F F# G G# A A# B".split()
MAJ = np.array([6.35, 2.23, 3.48, 2.33, 4.38, 4.09, 2.52, 5.19, 2.39, 3.66, 2.29, 2.88])
MIN = np.array([6.33, 2.68, 3.52, 5.38, 2.60, 3.53, 2.54, 4.75, 3.98, 2.69, 3.34, 3.17])
hz = lambda m: 440 * 2 ** ((m - 69) / 12)

def note(m, dur, amp=1.0):
    t = np.arange(int(fs * dur)) / fs
    tone = sum(np.sin(2 * np.pi * hz(m) * k * t) / k for k in range(1, 6))
    return amp * tone * np.minimum(1, t / 0.01) * np.exp(-t * 1.5)

def loop(chords, bass, sec=1.0):
    out = []
    for ch, b in zip(chords, bass):
        seg = sum(note(m, sec, 0.3) for m in ch) + note(b, sec, 0.5)
        out.append(seg)
    return np.concatenate(out)

def chroma(y):
    Y = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(y * np.hanning(y.size))) ** 2
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(y.size, 1 / fs); keep = (f > 50) & (f < 2000)
    pc = np.round(12 * np.log2(f[keep] / 440) + 69).astype(int) % 12
    c = np.bincount(pc, weights=Y[keep], minlength=12)
    return c / c.max()

def keys(c, n=3):
    sc = []
    for r in range(12):
        sc.append((np.corrcoef(c, np.roll(MAJ, r))[0, 1], f"{NAMES[r]} major"))
        sc.append((np.corrcoef(c, np.roll(MIN, r))[0, 1], f"{NAMES[r]} minor"))
    return sorted(sc, reverse=True)[:n]

def cents_off(y):
    Y = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(y * np.hanning(y.size))) ** 2
    f = np.fft.rfftfreq(y.size, 1 / fs); keep = (f > 50) & (f < 2000) & (Y > Y.max() * 1e-3)
    d = 2 * np.pi * (12 * np.log2(f[keep] / 440) % 1)            # position between semitones
    ang = np.angle(np.sum(Y[keep] * np.exp(1j * d)))
    return 100 * ang / (2 * np.pi)                                  # cents sharp (+) or flat (-) of A440 tuning

def show(label, y, want=None):
    print(f"   {label}: " + " | ".join(f"{k} {s:.3f}" for s, k in keys(chroma(y))))
    if want:
        allk = [k for _, k in keys(chroma(y), 24)]
        print(f"   {want} ranks #{allk.index(want) + 1} of 24")

Am, F, C, G = [57, 60, 64], [57, 60, 65], [55, 60, 64], [55, 59, 62]
print("1) Am-F-C-G loop, bass on each chord root (A2 F2 C3 G2)")
x = loop([Am, F, C, G], [45, 41, 48, 43]); show("whole loop", x, "A minor")
print(f"   tuning offset from A440 grid: {cents_off(x):+.0f} cents")
Dm, Em = [57, 62, 65], [55, 59, 64]
print("2) Am-Dm-Em-Am loop, bass A2 D3 E2 A2 (a loop that keeps coming home to A)")
show("whole loop", loop([Am, Dm, Em, Am], [45, 50, 40, 45]), "A minor")
print("3) Loop 1 slowed for a chopped-and-screwed flip")
for speed in (0.9, 0.8):
    idx = np.arange(0, x.size - 1, speed); y = np.interp(idx, np.arange(x.size), x)
    print(f"   {speed:.0%} speed = {12*np.log2(speed):+.2f} semitones")
    show(f"{speed:.0%} speed", y)
    print(f"   tuning offset from A440 grid: {cents_off(y):+.0f} cents")

Reading the numbers

Relative major and minor look almost the same. The Am–F–C–G loop came back C major with a score of 0.908. A minor, which uses the exact same seven notes, ranked fourth. Plenty of people would call that progression A minor because it starts on A. The detector can’t hear where a loop starts or lands, it just counts notes. For picking 808 notes that’s mostly harmless, since the notes are shared. For knowing where the bass should resolve, it’s the whole question.

Overtones can pull it the wrong way. The Am–Dm–Em–Am loop keeps coming home to A, and the detector still said E minor, with A minor seventh of 24. Every bass and chord note carries overtones, and the third harmonic of an A is an E. Stack enough of them and the detector starts hearing the fifth as home.

Slowed samples sit between keys. At 90% speed the loop dropped 1.82 semitones, not two. The detector snapped to the nearest key, A# major, but the notes were 18 cents sharp of it. At 80% speed it was 3.86 semitones down and 14 cents sharp of G# major. A detector that only names keys will never tell you that part. Tune an 808 straight to A# under the 90% loop and every long note sits 18 cents flat against the sample, which reads as a slow wobble rather than a wrong note.

A quicker way to be sure

  1. Run the detector and keep its top two or three answers, especially any relative major and minor pair.
  2. Loop the sample and hum or play the note that feels like the end of a sentence. Try it against the last bar. The one that sounds finished is your root.
  3. Check the tuning. If the sample came off a record that was sped up or slowed down, or you changed its speed yourself, find how many cents off it is and tune your instruments by that amount instead of rounding to the nearest key.
  4. Hold one long 808 note on the root under the loop. If it beats or wobbles against the sample, nudge the fine tune until it stops.

When the loop is busy, the home note is easier to hear with the drums and vocals out of the way. Pull the sample apart with StemSplit and listen to the bass or keys stem on its own; the root is usually obvious once it isn’t buried. Then go back to the full loop.

Write the key and the cents offset in your session notes. When you come back next month to add a bridge, you won’t have to work it out again.
How do I find the key of a sample?
Use a key detector for a shortlist, then pick the note the loop resolves to by ear. In our test the detector put the right key fourth for one loop and seventh for another.
Why does my key detector say major when the sample sounds minor?
Relative major and minor share all seven notes, so a note-counting detector often can’t tell them apart. Our Am–F–C–G loop came back C major.
Why does my 808 sound out of tune even in the right key?
The sample may sit between keys. Our loop slowed to 90% was 18 cents sharp of the A# major the detector reported, so an 808 on A# would beat against it.
How much does slowing a sample change its key?
Speed and pitch move together: semitones = 12 × log2(speed). 90% speed is −1.82 semitones and 80% is −3.86, so a slowed sample is rarely in a clean key.
