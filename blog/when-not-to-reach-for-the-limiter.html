Do you need a limiter on your master? When to skip it, and what to do instead

NoDAW Labs · Lacquer Vol. 02

You only need a limiter on your master when the peaks need catching. If the bounce already sits under your true-peak ceiling with no clipped samples, skip it. If true peak is over but nothing is clipped, one true-peak limiter at the very end does the job. And if the file is already clipped, already crushed, or the real problem is the arrangement, another limiter won’t help. It can cap peaks. It can’t give back the transients that are already gone.

Reaching for a limiter is a reflex for a lot of us. The master feels a bit small, so a limiter goes on, then the gain goes up, then it sounds louder, and louder sounds better for about thirty seconds. The trouble is that a limiter is a very specific tool. Using it on the wrong problem doesn’t just fail to help. It spends punch you won’t get back.

Do you actually need a limiter on your master?

Ask what job you want it to do. A limiter is a compressor with a very high ratio and a fast attack, set to stop the signal from going over a ceiling. That makes it great at exactly one thing: keeping peaks under a limit. Everything else people ask of it, like more energy, a bigger chorus or a mix that finally sounds finished, it does only by turning the loud parts down so you can turn the whole thing up.

That’s worth less than it used to be. Streaming services normalize playback, so a louder master is turned down to sit near everything else (the essay on why streaming turns loud masters down explains how). Loudness bought with limiting mostly comes back as lost punch.

What can a limiter fix, and what can’t it?

It can fix peaks that are too high. That’s it, and it’s plenty when it’s the actual problem.

It can’t fix:

  • Clipping that already happened. If samples were flattened at full scale somewhere upstream (an overdriven bus, a hot export, a plugin that clipped), the tops of those waveforms are gone. Clipping leaves flat tops and added distortion; a limiter can turn them down, but they stay flat.
  • A master that’s already crushed. When there’s very little gap left between the peaks and the average level, the fix is less gain, not a different limiter.
  • Problems that aren’t about level. An arrangement that never builds, a noisy intro, a click at an edit, a vocal buried under the hook. None of those are peak problems, so a peak tool can’t touch them.

Keep the difference between crest and loudness in mind too. A song can be loud and still dynamic, or quiet and still flat; the essay on crest factor vs loudness goes into why those are two different numbers.

How do you tell which problem you have?

Measure before you add anything. Four numbers sort it out:

  • Integrated loudness (LUFS), the average level over the song.
  • True peak (dBTP), the highest point of the waveform including the parts between samples.
  • Clipped samples, runs of consecutive samples pinned at the same maximum value: flat tops.
  • PLR, peak-to-loudness ratio: true peak minus integrated loudness. It’s a quick crest-factor read. A small number means the peaks have been pushed down close to the average.

The script below measures all four with FFmpeg’s ebur128 filter (which follows ITU-R BS.1770) plus a little numpy, and then names the cheapest fix. The thresholds in it, −1 dBTP and a PLR under 8 dB, are our starting points, not standards. Move them if your genre or your ears disagree.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""limiter_check.py: before you add another limiter, see which problem you actually have.
Usage: python3 limiter_check.py mix.wav [more.wav ...]
Needs FFmpeg on PATH plus numpy and soundfile (pip install numpy soundfile).
Reports integrated loudness and true peak (FFmpeg ebur128, ITU-R BS.1770), sample peak,
clipped samples (runs of 3+ consecutive samples pinned at the file's own peak, i.e. flat tops),
and PLR (true peak minus loudness, a quick crest-factor read). Then names the cheapest fix.
The thresholds (-1 dBTP ceiling, PLR under 8 dB = 'crushed') are our starting points, not standards."""
import sys, re, subprocess
import numpy as np, soundfile as sf

def ebur(p):
    err = subprocess.run(["ffmpeg", "-hide_banner", "-nostats", "-i", p, "-af", "ebur128=peak=true",
                          "-f", "null", "-"], capture_output=True, text=True).stderr
    return float(re.findall(r"I:\s+(-?[\d.]+) LUFS", err)[-1]), float(re.findall(r"Peak:\s+(-?[\d.]+) dBFS", err)[-1])

def flat_tops(x, tol=1e-6, run=3):
    pk = np.max(np.abs(x)); n = 0
    for ch in x.T:
        hit = np.abs(ch) >= pk - tol
        edges = np.diff(np.concatenate([[0], hit.astype(int), [0]]))
        starts, ends = np.where(edges == 1)[0], np.where(edges == -1)[0]
        n += int(((ends - starts) >= run).astype(int) @ (ends - starts))
    return n

for p in sys.argv[1:]:
    x, fs = sf.read(p, always_2d=True)
    I, tp = ebur(p); sp = 20 * np.log10(np.max(np.abs(x))); clips = flat_tops(x); plr = tp - I
    if clips: verdict = "clipped upstream: fix it in the session or the bounce; a limiter can't rebuild the tops"
    elif plr < 8: verdict = "already crushed: take gain out, don't add limiting"
    elif tp > -1.0: verdict = "peaks only: one true-peak cap at the very end is enough"
    else: verdict = "fine: no limiter needed for delivery"
    print(f"{p}\n  LUFS {I:6.1f} | true peak {tp:+5.1f} dBTP | sample peak {sp:+5.1f} dBFS | clipped samples {clips:6d} | PLR {plr:4.1f} dB\n  -> {verdict}")

Here’s what it printed on four synthetic versions of the same test beat, not real songs: one left with headroom, one driven into a sample-peak limiter, one pushed 6 dB into a hard clip, and one driven 10 dB into the limiter. They’re generated by make_material.py in the demo folder.

$ python3 limiter_check.py mix_ok.wav mix_hot_tp.wav mix_clipped.wav mix_crushed.wav

  -> fine: no limiter needed for delivery

  -> peaks only: one true-peak cap at the very end is enough
mix_clipped.wav
  LUFS   -6.3 | true peak  +3.0 dBTP | sample peak  +0.0 dBFS | clipped samples  53816 | PLR  9.3 dB
  -> clipped upstream: fix it in the session or the bounce; a limiter can't rebuild the tops
mix_crushed.wav
  LUFS   -6.5 | true peak  +0.7 dBTP | sample peak  -0.1 dBFS | clipped samples      0 | PLR  7.2 dB
  -> already crushed: take gain out, don't add limiting

What the four files are telling you:

  • mix_ok sits at −14.2 LUFS with a −2.6 dBTP true peak and no clips. It needs nothing. Adding a limiter here only changes the sound.
  • mix_hot_tp shows why sample peak lies. The limiter held the samples at −0.1 dBFS, but the true peak is +0.7 dBTP, because the waveform overshoots between samples. Nothing is clipped and the PLR is healthy, so one true-peak limiter at the end, or simply a little less gain, fixes it. The essay on true peak and codecs covers why those in-between overs matter once the file is encoded.
  • mix_clipped has 53,816 clipped samples and a +3.0 dBTP true peak. A limiter could pull the peak under the ceiling, but those flat tops would still be in the file. The honest fix is upstream: find where it clipped and bounce again.
  • mix_crushed has no clips and the same +0.7 dBTP true peak as mix_hot_tp, but it’s 2.2 LU louder with a PLR of only 7.2 dB. The answer here is to take gain out of the limiter that’s already on it, not to stack another one.

What should you do instead of another limiter?

Match the fix to the problem:

  1. Clipped samples: if you still have the session, fix it at the source. Pull down the track or bus that clipped, re-export, and measure again.
  2. True peak over, no clips: one final true-peak cap. That’s the only case where a limiter is the right answer.
  3. Loudness only slightly hot: turn it down. A fader move costs nothing.
  4. Already crushed: back off the drive on the limiter you have. If that makes it feel small, the fix is in the mix balance, not in more limiting.
  5. Not a level problem at all: go to the arrangement, the edit or the noise, and leave the master chain alone.

If you’re torn between a clipper and a limiter for that last bit of peak control, the clipper vs limiter essay measures the trade-off on the same drive.

Where does the limiter go if you do need one?

Last, after every move that changes level or tone, including the final loudness adjustment. Anything after it can push peaks back over. Use a true-peak or oversampled mode, since a sample-peak limiter can miss overs between samples, as mix_hot_tp just showed. For a ceiling, Spotify’s loudness guidance recommends staying below −1 dBTP, and below −2 dBTP if your master is louder than −14 LUFS, because louder tracks pick up more distortion when they’re encoded.

In the mix, stop earlier. A limiter on the master bus while you’re still balancing mostly hides the peaks you need to hear.

Do I need a limiter on my master?
Only if your peaks need catching. If the file already sits under your true-peak ceiling (−1 dBTP is a common one) and has no clipped samples, you can deliver it without a limiter. If true peak is over and nothing is clipped, one true-peak limiter at the very end is enough.
Can a limiter fix clipping?
No. Once samples are flattened at full scale, the original tops of the waveform are gone. A limiter can turn the file down and stop new overs, but the flat tops stay flat. Fix clipping where it happened: in the session, on the track, or on the bus that clipped, then bounce again.
How do I know if I’m over-limiting?
Listen for drums that stop hitting, a vocal that pumps with the kick, and a chorus that feels no bigger than the verse. On the meter, a small gap between true peak and integrated loudness (PLR) is a hint; we start paying attention below about 8 dB. Taking gain out usually sounds better than any setting change.
Should the limiter be the last thing in the chain?
For delivery, yes. Put the true-peak cap after anything that changes level or tone, including the final loudness move, so nothing after it can push peaks back over. During mixing, a limiter on the master bus mostly hides problems you’d rather hear.
What ceiling should I set on my limiter?
Use a true-peak (oversampled) mode and set about −1 dBTP. Spotify recommends staying below −1 dBTP, and below −2 dBTP if your master is louder than −14 LUFS, because louder tracks pick up more distortion when they’re encoded for streaming.
